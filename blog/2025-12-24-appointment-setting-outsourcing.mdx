Your account executives are expensive. Every hour they spend researching prospects, dialing numbers and chasing replies is an hour they are not spending on the conversations that close deals. Appointment setting moves the first part of that work to a dedicated team, so your closers start their day with meetings already on the calendar.

Done well, it is one of the most measurable things you can outsource. Done badly, it fills calendars with meetings that never should have happened. The difference is almost entirely in how you define a good appointment.

## How it works

A typical outsourced appointment setting operation has four steps.

1. **List building.** Prospects matching your ideal customer profile are identified and researched, either from lists you provide or from sources the team builds.
2. **Outreach.** Agents contact prospects by phone, usually combined with email and LinkedIn touches in a planned sequence.
3. **Qualification.** On a live conversation, the agent confirms the prospect fits your criteria and has a reason to talk.
4. **Booking and confirmation.** The meeting goes straight into your rep's calendar with notes, followed by a confirmation and a reminder.

The setter's job ends at a confirmed meeting with a qualified prospect. Selling is your team's job. Mixing the two, asking setters to pitch hard or reps to cold call, usually makes both worse.

## Define a qualified appointment before anything else

The most important document in an appointment setting program is one paragraph long: the definition of a meeting you are willing to pay for.

A useful definition covers:

- **Fit:** company size, industry, location, technology or any other hard criteria
- **Role:** the job titles or responsibilities of the person who must attend
- **Need:** at least one confirmed reason the meeting is worth their time, in their words
- **Timing:** whether they are evaluating now, this quarter, or simply curious

Many teams use a simple framework like BANT (budget, authority, need, timeline) as a starting point. Adapt it to how your customers actually buy. For a low-cost product, asking about budget on a first call is unnecessary friction. For an enterprise deal, not knowing who has authority wastes everyone's time.

Write the definition down, agree on it with your sales team, and give examples of meetings that pass and meetings that do not. Ambiguity here is the root of almost every dispute between sales teams and appointment setters.

## The metrics that matter

| Metric | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Meetings booked | Raw output, useful only alongside the next three |
| Qualification acceptance rate | Share of booked meetings your reps agree met the definition |
| Show rate | Share of booked meetings that actually happen |
| Meeting-to-opportunity rate | Share of held meetings that become real sales opportunities |
| Cost per qualified meeting held | Total cost divided by meetings that happened and qualified |

The last one is the number to manage to. A program booking forty meetings a month with half of them no-shows and unqualified is worse than one booking twenty that all happen and fit.

**Show rate** deserves special attention. It is mostly controlled by what happens after booking: a clear calendar invite with the agenda, a confirmation email in the prospect's own words, and a reminder the day before. Programs that skip these routinely lose a large share of their meetings.

## Feedback is the engine

Appointment setting improves only when setters hear what happened to the meetings they booked. Build a simple loop:

- Reps mark every meeting as held or not held, and qualified or not qualified, within a day
- Disputed meetings get a short reason in writing
- Once a week, the setting team reviews the reasons and adjusts targeting or qualification questions

Without this loop, setters keep booking the same kind of bad meeting because nobody told them it was bad.

## Compliance is not optional

Outbound calling is regulated. In the US, telemarketing rules restrict calling hours and require screening against the national Do Not Call Registry for consumer calls; in the UK, marketing calls must be screened against the Telephone Preference Service, and similar rules exist across Europe. B2B calling is often treated differently from consumer calling, but not everywhere and not always. Your provider should be able to explain exactly which rules apply to your campaign.

## Pricing models

Providers usually charge in one of three ways:

- **Per hour or per agent:** predictable cost; you carry the performance risk
- **Per appointment:** you pay for results; watch carefully for quality, because the incentive is volume
- **Hybrid:** a lower base fee plus a bonus for qualified meetings held

Per-appointment pricing sounds safest but only works if "appointment" means a qualified meeting that actually happened, not one that was merely booked.
