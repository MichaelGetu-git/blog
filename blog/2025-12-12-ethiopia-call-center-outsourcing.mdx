When companies think about offshore call centers, the Philippines and India come to mind first, with Latin America close behind for US clients. Ethiopia is rarely on the first list. It is increasingly on the second one, the list companies make after their first provider stops delivering.

That is not a coincidence. The things that make a call center work, people who want the job and are good at it, the right hours and a reliable setup, are exactly where Ethiopia has improved fastest. Here is an honest look at the case, including the parts you should check before you commit.

## The people

Call center quality is mostly a hiring problem. You need agents who communicate clearly, stay patient with frustrated callers, follow process and want to keep the job long enough to get good at it.

Ethiopia has a large, young and growing workforce, and every year far more university graduates enter the job market than there are professional roles for them locally. For a call center, that means you can hire selectively. You are not taking whoever is available; you are picking the strongest candidates from a deep pool.

English is the language of instruction in Ethiopian secondary schools and universities, so graduates have spent years studying, writing and presenting in it. Spoken fluency and accent still vary from person to person, as they do everywhere. The right approach is to test every candidate on a live call before hiring rather than relying on any general claim about a country, including this one.

Many agents also speak Amharic, Afaan Oromo, Tigrinya or Somali, which matters if you serve East African customers or diaspora communities.

## The hours

Ethiopia runs on East Africa Time, UTC+3, and never changes its clocks. That gives it an unusual spread of useful overlaps:

- **UK and Europe:** Ethiopia is one to three hours ahead, so a normal Ethiopian working day covers almost the entire European business day. No night shifts needed.
- **Gulf states:** one hour behind Dubai, so the working days line up almost exactly.
- **United States:** seven to eight hours ahead of New York. A normal daytime shift in Addis Ababa covers the US overnight, and an afternoon-to-evening shift overlaps the US morning.

For US companies, the most common pattern is overnight and early-morning coverage, so your customers wake up to answers. Combined with a domestic team, that gives you round-the-clock cover without anyone working a graveyard shift they hate.

## The infrastructure

Five years ago, connectivity was the strongest argument against Ethiopia. It is now a manageable one. The telecom market opened to competition when Safaricom launched in 2022, and mobile data quality and coverage improved quickly, especially in Addis Ababa. Fiber broadband is widely available in the capital.

Power remains the thing to plan for. Outages happen, so every agent working for a serious provider should have backup power, typically an inverter or battery, and a second connection, usually mobile data. Ask how this is verified, not just whether it exists. "We check the setup before placement and test failover" is the answer you want.

## The cost

Labor costs in Ethiopia are a fraction of those in the US, UK or Western Europe, and meaningfully lower than in more established offshore markets. That gap matters less for its own sake than for what it lets you do: hire better-qualified agents, staff more generously at peak hours, or pay for proper team leads and QA instead of stretching a skeleton crew.

The common mistake is to treat a lower cost base as permission to cut corners. The companies that do best in Ethiopia use the savings to raise quality, not just to lower the bill.

## What to check before you commit

Ethiopia is a strong option, not a guaranteed one. Before signing with any provider, confirm:

- **How agents are recruited and tested,** including live English calls and a realistic role-play
- **Backup power and internet** for every agent, verified rather than promised
- **Public holiday coverage.** Ethiopia follows its own calendar, with holidays such as Genna in January and Enkutatash and Meskel in September. Make sure your coverage plan accounts for them
- **Data protection.** How call recordings, customer data and access to your systems are handled and who can see them
- **Reporting.** What you will see each week and how quickly problems are raised
- **Contract terms.** Notice periods, replacement guarantees if an agent leaves, and who employs the agents legally

Then ask how they measure performance. A provider who cannot explain how they track quality cannot tell you whether they are delivering it; our guide to [call center KPIs that actually matter](https://zemenaytech.com/blog/call-center-kpis-that-matter) lists the numbers worth asking for.

## Where it works best

In our experience, Ethiopian call center teams are strongest in:

- **Customer support** for software, e-commerce and subscription businesses
- **Overnight and weekend coverage** for US companies
- **Full business-day coverage** for UK, European and Gulf companies
- **Outbound lead qualification and appointment setting** for B2B sales teams
- **Order management and follow-up** for retail and logistics

It works less well for roles that need deep local knowledge of a specific market, such as regulated financial advice, or native-level slang in a particular regional accent. Be honest about which kind of role yours is.

## Getting started

The lowest-risk way to test Ethiopia is a small pilot: two or three agents on a well-defined queue, measured against your current team for a month. If the numbers hold, scale. If they do not, you have learned something cheaply.
