Customers do not keep office hours. An e-commerce order goes wrong at 11pm, a software outage hits on Sunday morning, a traveler loses a booking at 3am in a different country. If you sell to people across time zones, or simply sell to people who are busy during the day, at some point you will need support that never closes.

The usual way to get there is to ask your existing team to stretch: longer shifts, rotating nights, someone on call for weekends. It works for a few months. Then your best agents start leaving, and the ones who stay are tired enough that quality drops at exactly the hours you were trying to cover.

There is a better way to build it.

## Start with the demand, not the clock

Before you design shifts, find out when customers actually contact you. Pull call, chat and email volume by hour for the last three months and plot it. Almost every business finds the same shape: a busy daytime peak, a long tail into the evening, and a quiet overnight period that is never quite empty.

That shape tells you three things:

- **How many people you need at peak** to hit your service level
- **How many people you need overnight**, which is often one or two, not a full team
- **Which hours could be handled differently**, for example by chat and email with slower response targets, rather than live phone cover

Many businesses discover that "24/7" really means full phone cover for sixteen hours and a smaller, multi-channel team for the other eight. That is a much easier problem to staff.

## Use geography instead of night shifts

The healthiest way to cover the night is to have it be daytime for somebody. This is the idea behind follow-the-sun support: hand the queue between teams in different time zones so nobody works through their own night.

For a US business, an Ethiopian team is an unusually good fit. Ethiopia is seven to eight hours ahead of New York, so an ordinary daytime shift in Addis Ababa covers the entire US overnight period, and nobody on either side works through their own night. For UK and European businesses, the same team covers the full business day, and an afternoon-to-evening shift extends cover well into the European evening.

A simple two-region pattern for a US company might look like this:

| Hours (US Eastern) | Covered by |
| --- | --- |
| Midnight to 8am | Ethiopian daytime shift, roughly 7am to 4pm in Addis Ababa |
| 8am to 4pm | Domestic team, plus an Ethiopian afternoon shift at peak |
| 4pm to midnight | Domestic evening shift |

Two well-run regions are usually enough. Three or more add handover complexity that only very large operations need.

## If you do need night shifts

Sometimes geography is not an option, or you need native coverage for a specific market. In that case, design night work deliberately:

- **Fixed nights beat rotating nights.** People who choose night work and keep a stable schedule cope far better than people rotated between days and nights every week.
- **Pay a real night differential.** It is the cheapest retention tool you have.
- **Keep overnight teams small and senior.** With low volume and less backup, overnight agents need to solve problems on their own.
- **Give them a clear escalation path** for genuine emergencies, and a defined list of what can wait until morning.

## Watch occupancy, not just coverage

The fastest way to burn out a support team is to keep it busy every single minute. Occupancy, the share of logged-in time spent actually handling contacts, should leave room to breathe. Sustained occupancy above roughly 90% means agents go straight from one difficult call to the next with no recovery, and you will see it later in sick days, short tempers and resignations.

Schedule real breaks, protect time for training and quality reviews, and staff a little above the minimum your service-level calculation says you need. The extra cost is smaller than the cost of replacing agents. ## Make handovers boring

In a multi-region setup, handovers are where things get lost. A customer explains their problem to one team at 5pm and has to explain it again to another team at 6pm. Avoid that with:

- **One shared ticketing system** that every region uses, with full history on every case
- **A written handover note** at each shift change: open escalations, known incidents, anything unusual
- **Clear ownership rules,** for example a case stays with the agent who opened it until it is resolved or formally handed over
- **A single knowledge base** so every region gives the same answer to the same question

## Measure each region the same way

Report service level, resolution rate and CSAT by region and by hour. If the overnight team's numbers are consistently worse, find out why before blaming the people: overnight agents often have less access to specialists, older documentation and fewer tools. Fix the support around them first.
