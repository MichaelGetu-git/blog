Everyone has been on the receiving end of a bad script. The agent asks how your day is going in a voice that makes clear they do not care, reads a paragraph about the company's commitment to excellence, and then answers a question you did not ask. You can hear the page.

The fix is not to throw scripts away. Untrained agents without structure are worse. The fix is to stop writing scripts as speeches and start writing them as maps.

## Scripts versus call flows

A traditional script is a block of text the agent reads. A call flow is a structure: the stages a call moves through, the goal of each stage, and the few phrases that must be said exactly. Everything in between is the agent's own words.

Most good call flows have five stages:

1. **Opening:** who you are, why you are calling or how you can help, and permission to continue
2. **Discovery:** questions that find out what the customer actually needs
3. **Solution or pitch:** the answer, offer or next step that fits what discovery found
4. **Objections and questions:** handling hesitation without pressure
5. **Close and next step:** what happens now, confirmed out loud

For each stage, write the goal in one sentence. "Discovery: find out whether they handle more than 200 support tickets a month and who decides on tools." An agent who understands the goal can get there in their own voice. An agent who only has the words is stuck the moment the customer goes off-script, which happens on almost every call.

## What to write word for word

Some things should be exact, and agents should know why.

- **Legal and compliance statements:** call recording notices, required disclosures, consent language
- **Verification steps:** the questions that confirm identity before discussing an account
- **Prices, terms and promises:** anything that becomes a commitment when spoken
- **The opening line**, at least for new agents, because the first five seconds decide whether the call continues

Mark these clearly in the flow, in a different color or with a label. Everything else is guidance.

## Write talk tracks, not paragraphs

For the flexible parts, give agents talk tracks: two or three ways of saying the same thing, written the way people speak. Read them aloud before you publish them. If a sentence makes you run out of breath or sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

Compare:

> "Zemenay is a leading provider of world-class remote staffing solutions committed to delivering exceptional value."

> "We help companies hire remote staff in Ethiopia. Most of our clients come to us because they need people fast and can't find them locally."

The second version is shorter, specific and sounds like a person. It also invites a response, which is the point of a conversation.

## Build the discovery questions first

Weak scripts spend most of their words on the pitch. Strong ones spend them on questions, because the answers decide what to say next.

Good discovery questions are open, specific and easy to answer:

- "How are you handling this today?"
- "What happens when that goes wrong?"
- "Who else is involved when you make a decision like this?"
- "If you could fix one part of it this quarter, which would it be?"

Give agents a short list and let them choose the order. Then teach them the most underrated skill in calling: after asking, stop talking.

## Objections: a library, not a rebuttal

List the ten objections your agents hear most. Your team leads already know them. For each, write:

- **What it usually means.** "Send me an email" often means "I'm busy right now", not "I'm interested."
- **One clarifying question.** "Happy to. So I send the right thing, what would you want it to cover?"
- **One honest response.** Not a trick, just the most useful true thing to say.
- **When to let go.** Pressure after a clear no damages your brand and, in many markets, breaks the rules.

Keep the library in a shared document and update it monthly. Objections change as your market and your competitors change.

## Train with role-play, not reading

A call flow learned by reading is forgotten by lunch. Run role-plays where a team lead plays a difficult customer and the agent works through the flow. Record them, review them together, and repeat until the agent can handle the common paths without looking at the page.

Then listen to real calls. The best talk tracks usually come from your best agents improvising something that worked. When you hear one, add it to the flow and credit the agent who said it.

## Measure the script, not just the agents

If every agent struggles at the same stage, the problem is the flow. Watch where calls drop, where objections cluster and where conversion falls off.
