A development team spread across time zones can be a huge advantage. Work moves forward while you sleep, you can hire the best people wherever they live, and an offshore team can cost far less than a local one. It can also be miserable: questions that wait a day for an answer, meetings at midnight, and a growing sense that nobody quite knows what anyone else is doing.

The difference is rarely the people. It is the way the team communicates. Here are the rituals that make distributed development work.

## Protect a small overlap, then go async

Most teams need some time when everyone is online, but far less than they think. Two hours of overlap a day is enough for most distributed teams, as long as everything else is designed to work without real-time conversation.

Find the overlap and protect it:

- **Use it for conversation,** not status updates: unblocking questions, design discussions, pairing, and the occasional team call
- **Keep it free of solo work** where possible, so people are available
- **Rotate the inconvenience** if the overlap falls early or late for someone; do not make the same people always take the awkward hours

For a team in Ethiopia working with Europe, overlap is easy: almost the entire working day lines up. With the US East Coast, Ethiopia is seven to eight hours ahead, so the Ethiopian late afternoon matches the American morning. That is a natural window for a short daily call.

## Replace the standup with a written update

The live daily standup is the first meeting to cut. Replace it with a short written update posted at the end of each person's day, in a dedicated channel:

- **Done:** what I finished today, with links
- **Next:** what I will work on tomorrow
- **Blocked:** anything I need from someone, and from whom

Written updates create a record, respect everyone's hours, and are faster to read than a meeting is to attend. The person waking up in another time zone starts the day knowing exactly where things stand.

## Write things down, properly

In a distributed team, writing is the main way work gets done. Invest in it:

- **Tickets with full context.** A good ticket explains the problem, the goal, the acceptance criteria and any relevant links, so a developer can start without waiting for an answer.
- **Decision records.** When an important technical or product decision is made, write a short note: what was decided, the options considered and why. It saves the same discussion happening again in three months.
- **A living project README** covering how to set up the project, deploy it and find things.

The test of good written communication is simple: could someone in another time zone act on this without asking a follow-up question?

## Make code review fast and kind

Code review is where distributed teams most often stall. A pull request opened at the end of one person's day and reviewed at the start of another's can lose a full day each round.

- **Keep pull requests small,** so they can be reviewed in minutes, not hours
- **Agree on a review window,** for example every PR is reviewed within one working day, and the overlap hours are used for anything urgent
- **Explain the why** in the PR description, with screenshots or a short video for visual changes
- **Distinguish blockers from suggestions** in review comments, so the author knows what must change before merging

## Use the time difference for handoffs

The time zone gap can speed work up. A developer in Addis Ababa finishes a feature as the US team starts its day; the US product manager reviews it and leaves feedback by their afternoon; the developer picks it up the next morning. With clear handoff notes, work moves forward almost around the clock.

This works best with well-defined tasks and good testing environments, where reviewers can try changes without asking the developer how.

## Hold one meaningful meeting a week

Cutting status meetings frees time for the meetings that matter. A weekly or fortnightly session in the overlap window should cover:

- A demo of what was finished, by the people who built it
- Priorities for the next week or sprint
- Problems, risks and decisions that need everyone

Monthly or quarterly, add something less transactional: a retrospective, a technical deep dive, or simply a call with no agenda. Remote teams lose the hallway conversations that build trust, so they need to create a few on purpose.

## Measure outcomes, not hours online

The worst habit in remote management is measuring presence: green dots in chat, keyboard activity, instant replies. It drives good developers away and says nothing about results. Measure what the team delivers instead: features shipped, quality, predictability against estimates, and how quickly problems are fixed.
