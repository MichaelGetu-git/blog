Most businesses know exactly when their busiest weeks will be. Retailers have the holiday season, travel companies have summer and school breaks, accountants have tax season, software companies have their annual renewal cycle and every product launch. And yet peak season catches support teams out every year: queues balloon, hold times stretch, and the business ends up hiring in a panic three weeks too late.

Seasonal peaks are not a surprise. They are a planning problem. Here is how to solve it before the phones start ringing.

## Forecast from your own history

Start with last year's volume by week and by hour, across every channel. Then adjust for what has changed:

- **Growth:** if your customer base is a third larger than last year, peak volume probably is too
- **Known events:** launches, price changes, marketing campaigns, shipping cut-off dates
- **Product changes** that will create new questions, or fix old ones
- **Channel shifts,** such as more customers using chat instead of phone

The output you need is a forecast of contacts per hour for each week of the peak, not a single seasonal multiplier. Peaks are uneven; the Monday after a holiday weekend is usually far worse than the week average suggests.

## Turn the forecast into headcount

Convert forecast volume into the number of agents needed per hour to hit your service level, using your average handle time. Workforce management tools do this with Erlang calculations; a spreadsheet works for smaller teams. Then add a realistic allowance for breaks, training, absence and the slower handling times of new agents.

The gap between that number and your current team is your capacity problem, hour by hour. It is usually smaller than people fear in total and much larger than expected in a few specific hours.

## Five ways to add capacity

You rarely need to solve the whole gap with new hires. Combine these levers.

**1. Reduce demand before it arrives.** Every contact you prevent is cheaper than one you staff for. Before peak season, review last year's top contact reasons and fix what you can: clearer delivery dates on the website, proactive shipping updates, a better returns page, an FAQ article for the five most common questions.

**2. Move volume to cheaper channels.** Promote self-service and chat during peak periods; one chat agent can usually handle more conversations per hour than a phone agent handles calls.

**3. Adjust schedules.** Cancel non-essential training and meetings during peak weeks, offer voluntary overtime fairly, and move shift start times to match the forecast curve.

**4. Extend hours with another time zone.** A team in a different time zone can absorb evening and overnight volume, which during a peak often includes the overflow from the day. We describe the pattern in [running 24/7 support without burning out your team](https://zemenaytech.com/blog/24-7-customer-support-without-burnout).

**5. Add temporary or outsourced agents.** For the remaining gap, bring in seasonal agents, either directly or through a provider who can scale a team up for the peak and back down afterwards.

## Ramp temporary agents properly

Seasonal agents fail when they are thrown onto the full queue with a day of training. Give them a narrower job instead:

- **Route only the simplest, highest-volume contact types** to new agents, such as order status, delivery dates and basic account questions
- **Train them on those types only,** using a short version of your normal onboarding
- **Keep your experienced agents on complex issues** and escalations
- **Give new agents a quick escalation path** to an experienced buddy

This tiered approach means new agents become useful within days rather than weeks. Our [30-day call center onboarding plan](https://zemenaytech.com/blog/onboarding-an-outsourced-call-center-team) can be compressed into a week or two for narrowly scoped seasonal roles.

## Start earlier than feels necessary

Work back from the first day of peak volume:

| When | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 10 to 12 weeks before | Finalize the forecast and the capacity gap |
| 8 weeks before | Agree on outsourced or seasonal capacity; start recruiting |
| 4 to 6 weeks before | Train seasonal agents; fix top contact drivers on the website |
| 2 weeks before | Seasonal agents live on simple queues, at normal volume |
| Peak | Daily volume reviews, flexible scheduling, fast escalations |
| 2 weeks after | Review what happened while it is fresh |

The two weeks of live calls before the peak are the step most often skipped and the most valuable. Agents who have handled real calls at normal volume cope far better when the surge arrives.

## Protect quality during the rush

Quality usually slips at peak because QA and coaching are the first things cancelled. Keep a lighter version going: fewer calls reviewed per agent, but every agent reviewed every week. Watch first call resolution closely; if it drops, repeat contacts will add to the volume you are already struggling with. The core measures are in [call center KPIs that actually matter](https://zemenaytech.com/blog/call-center-kpis-that-matter).

## Review it afterwards

Within two weeks of the peak ending, compare forecast against actual volume by hour, list the contact reasons that surprised you, and note what you would change. That document is next year's head start.
