When you outsource software or website development, the contract model shapes the project as much as the people do. The same team, building the same product, will behave differently on a fixed-price contract than on a time-and-materials one. Choose a model that does not fit your project and you will spend months negotiating change requests instead of building.

There are three common models. None is best in general. Each is best for a particular kind of work.

## Fixed price

You agree on a defined scope, a price and a deadline. The provider is responsible for delivering that scope for that price.

**How it works in practice:** the provider estimates the work in detail, adds a margin for risk, and commits. Anything outside the agreed scope becomes a change request, priced separately.

**Advantages:**

- Your budget is known in advance
- The provider carries the risk of underestimating
- It forces both sides to define requirements clearly up front

**Disadvantages:**

- You pay for the provider's risk margin, even if nothing goes wrong
- Changing your mind is expensive and slow
- Providers are motivated to interpret scope narrowly
- Writing a detailed enough specification takes time before any work starts

**Best for:** well-defined projects with stable requirements, such as a marketing website with an approved design, a specific integration, or a clearly scoped feature. If you cannot describe the finished product in detail today, fixed price will hurt.

## Time and materials

You pay for the hours or days actually worked, usually at agreed rates per role, and the scope can evolve as you go.

**How it works in practice:** work is planned in short cycles, often one- or two-week sprints. You set priorities, the team delivers, and you adjust the plan based on what you learn.

**Advantages:**

- Full flexibility to change priorities and requirements
- No risk margin built into the price
- Work can start quickly without an exhaustive specification
- You see progress continuously and can stop at any point

**Disadvantages:**

- The final cost is not fixed, so budget discipline is your job
- It requires more of your time to set priorities and review work
- It depends on trusting the provider's efficiency and honest reporting

**Best for:** products where requirements will change as you learn, such as MVPs, new features based on user feedback, or projects with technical unknowns. Protect yourself with a budget cap per phase, regular demos and transparent time reporting.

## Dedicated team

You get developers, and sometimes designers, testers and a project manager, who work only on your product, typically for a monthly fee per person. You direct the work, as you would with your own staff.

**How it works in practice:** the provider recruits, employs and supports the team; you manage priorities and day-to-day work. The team joins your tools, your meetings and your codebase. This is often called staff augmentation.

**Advantages:**

- Deep product knowledge builds up over time
- Predictable monthly cost
- Full control over priorities and ways of working
- Much faster to scale than hiring locally

**Disadvantages:**

- You need someone able to lead the work technically and set priorities
- It is a longer-term commitment than a single project
- Productivity depends on how well you integrate the team

**Best for:** ongoing product development, long roadmaps, and companies that want engineering capacity rather than a single deliverable.

## Side by side

| | Fixed price | Time and materials | Dedicated team |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Budget certainty | High | Medium, with caps | High per month |
| Flexibility | Low | High | High |
| Your time required | Low after spec | Medium | High |
| Who manages the work | Provider | Shared | You |
| Best project length | Weeks to a few months | Any | Months to years |
| Typical use | Defined websites, features | MVPs, evolving products | Ongoing product teams |

## Combining models

Many successful projects use more than one model over their life:

1. **A small fixed-price discovery phase** to define requirements, architecture and estimates
2. **Time and materials for the first version,** while the product is still changing quickly
3. **A dedicated team for ongoing development** once the roadmap is long and stable

This approach limits your risk at the start, keeps flexibility when you need it most, and builds a stable team once you know what you are building.

## Questions to ask any provider

- How do you estimate, and how accurate were your last few estimates?
- How are change requests handled and priced?
- What reporting will I see each week?
- Who owns the code, and where does it live during the project?
- What happens if a team member leaves?
