Quality assurance is supposed to make calls better. In many call centers it does the opposite. Agents learn that QA means a reviewer listening for mistakes, a score they do not understand, and a meeting where they are told what they did wrong. So they optimize for the scorecard rather than the customer, and the scorecard slowly drifts away from anything customers would recognize as good service.

A QA program that works looks different. Agents understand the scorecard, agree that it describes a good call, and see reviews as the fastest way to get better. Here is how to build one.

## Start from what customers value

Before writing a single scorecard item, list what makes a call good from the customer's point of view. For most support calls it comes down to five things:

1. The problem was understood quickly
2. The answer was correct
3. It was resolved without being passed around
4. The agent was polite and seemed to care
5. The customer knows what happens next

Then add what the business needs: accurate records, compliance statements, correct verification, proper use of systems. Every item on the scorecard should trace back to one of these two lists. If an item does not, remove it.

## Keep the scorecard short

The most common QA mistake is a scorecard with forty items. Reviewers rush through it, agents cannot remember it, and important behaviors get the same weight as trivia like saying the company name twice.

Aim for ten to fifteen items grouped into a few sections:

| Section | Example items |
| --- | --- |
| Opening | Correct greeting, verification completed, recording notice given |
| Understanding | Asked clarifying questions, confirmed the issue back to the customer |
| Resolution | Correct information, resolved or escalated appropriately, no unnecessary transfer |
| Communication | Clear, polite, avoided jargon, managed holds properly |
| Wrap-up | Next steps confirmed, accurate notes in the CRM |

## Separate critical errors from everything else

Some mistakes matter far more than others. Giving a customer wrong information about a refund, skipping identity verification, or breaking a calling rule should not cost the same few points as forgetting to use the customer's name.

Mark a small number of items as critical. A critical failure means the call fails QA regardless of the other scores, and it triggers immediate coaching. Everything else is scored normally. This keeps the overall score meaningful and makes clear to everyone which behaviors are non-negotiable.

## Write definitions, not labels

"Showed empathy" means different things to different reviewers. Every item needs a short definition of what a pass looks like, with an example:

> **Confirmed the issue back to the customer.** Pass: the agent summarized the problem in their own words and the customer agreed before the agent started solving it. "So the charge on the 3rd is the one you don't recognize, is that right?"

Definitions are what make scores consistent between reviewers, and they are what make the scorecard teachable.

## Calibrate every week

Calibration is the single most important QA habit. Once a week, reviewers and team leads score the same two or three calls independently, then compare. Where scores differ, discuss until you agree, and update the definitions if they were unclear.

Include agents in calibration sessions regularly. Nothing builds trust in a scorecard faster than agents seeing how reviewers think, and agents often point out when an item does not match what customers actually respond to.

## Sample fairly

Score enough calls to be fair, typically a few per agent per week, and choose them randomly across call types and times of day. Do not only review complaints or long calls; that gives a distorted picture and teaches agents that QA is a punishment. Add targeted reviews on top of the random sample when you are investigating something specific.

## Turn scores into coaching

A score with no conversation attached changes nothing. Every review should lead to a short coaching discussion:

- **Start with what went well,** specifically. "Your summary of the problem at the two-minute mark was exactly right."
- **Pick one thing to improve,** not five. Agents can change one behavior at a time.
- **Listen to the moment together** rather than describing it.
- **Agree on what to try next time,** and check it in the next review.

Track coaching outcomes as well as scores. If an agent's score on one item does not improve after coaching, the problem may be the training, the process or the tools rather than the agent.

## Connect QA to the other numbers

QA scores should line up with your customer-facing metrics. If QA scores are high but first call resolution and CSAT are low, the scorecard is measuring the wrong things.
